Hybrid course 10H · International audience · Inequalities — economic history and thought

This syllabus is an open proposal. Each partner instructor is invited to complete, adapt, or enrich it according to their national context.

Session 1 — Why study the economic history of inequalities?

Main ideas

  • Economic history distinguishes structural trends from cyclical fluctuations: contemporary inequalities are not inevitable — they have a temporal depth.
  • The long view as a tool for analytical decentring: comparing across time and space forces us to relativise our analytical categories.
  • Inequalities are multidimensional — income, wealth, opportunity, gender, territory — and every measure implies a normative choice: the Gini is not neutral.

Women’s Thread — Session 1

Claudia Goldin (Nobel 2023) shows that women’s labour market participation over two centuries follows a U-shaped curve — a fact invisible to standard aggregate measures. The child penalty is today the main factor in the gender pay gap.

Pedagogical proposals

  • Opening discussion: why economic history rather than theory alone? “History is the only serious way to do economics” — Piketty
  • Round table: each student estimates the Gini of their country, then compare with real data (wid.world) — surprise as entry point
  • Comparative presentation of indicators: Gini, Palma, D9/D1 — discussion of limits
  • Normative positioning exercise: Rawls vs Nozick in small groups

Session 2 — From Antiquity to the Renaissance: the first justifications

  • Acts I, II, III as reading material
  • Comparison: natural inequality (Aristotle) / sacred inequality (Aquinas) / economic inequality (Smith)
  • The question of blind spots: slavery, Atlantic trade, women
  • Seminar: reread Rousseau on natural vs political inequality

Pedagogical proposals

  • Critical reading: an excerpt from Aristotle Politics Book I (chapters 4–7) — natural slavery
  • Debate: “Was Smith a liberal or a social critic?” — using the Book V self-critique
  • Assign: find one present-day application of the ‘natural hierarchy’ argument

Session 3 — 19th and 20th centuries: conflict and compression

  • Acts IV and V as reading material
  • From Ricardo to Marx: from the iron law of wages to surplus value
  • The Glorious Thirty as historical exception: conditions and limits
  • Introduction to the r > g formula

Women’s Thread — Session 3

Joan Robinson (1903–1983) and Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919): two theoretical contributions that shaped 20th century economics, both systematically marginalised.

Pedagogical proposals

  • Data workshop: r > g chart (Act V) — identify the Glorious Thirty parenthesis
  • Pair comparison: Hayek vs Keynes on the 1968 context (Thomas’s Decision, Act V)
  • Reading: Piketty introduction to Capital in the Twenty-First Century, ch. 1

Session 4 — The 21st century: measuring without being able to reduce?

  • Act VI as reading material
  • Milanovic’s elephant curve: global winners and losers
  • The child penalty and gender inequalities (Goldin)
  • Platform economy and new forms of precarity

Pedagogical proposals

  • Data workshop: elephant curve (Act VI) — identify your country’s position
  • Structured debate: Debate III on capital taxation (page Structured Debates)
  • Assignment: research the Gini of your country of origin on WID.world — compare with course data

Session 5 — Synthesis: what is to be done?

  • Full pathway completed
  • Logbook presentation and export
  • Student ideological profiles (My Progress page)
  • Final open question: “What is to be done?”

Pedagogical proposals

  • Each student shares one logbook entry (2 min) — collective listening
  • Mapping ideological profiles: which thinker was most cited? Which position was most often taken?
  • Final debate: “Is economic justice achievable?” — using all acts
  • Portfolio submission: exported logbook + self-assessment sheet
