Logbook · Lyon, July 1555
Lyon is the economic capital of the kingdom. Italian, German and Flemish merchants cross each other on the docks. The bill of exchange replaces gold — we trade promises, not metals. I met a woman poet, Louise Labé, daughter of an enriched ropemaker. She writes about love with the same precision as an accountant with his ledgers. And she is free, in her own way — because her father had money.
Economic note The Lyon fair sees the equivalent of 50 tonnes of silver pass through it annually in bills of exchange. Meanwhile, 12 million Africans are deported to the Americas between 1500 and 1800. European wealth and the Atlantic slave trade are inseparable — but no Enlightenment economist will say so clearly.
Economic progress creates new freedoms for some — and new forms of servitude for others. The Enlightenment's blind spot is precisely what it chooses not to look at.

How inequality is perceived

From mercantilism to the Enlightenment, inequality changes justification: no longer divine, it becomes economic. It is the mechanical result of the division of labour, trade and private property.

But this rationalisation leaves immense blind spots: the Atlantic slave trade (12 million deported), the enclosure of commons in England, the exclusion of women from economic citizenship.

Women’s Thread — Act III

Louise Labé (1524–1566), Lyonnaise poet, embodies the contradiction: exceptional intellectual freedom, acquired through her father’s merchant wealth, in a society that excluded women from guilds, universities and official commerce. Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797) would be the first to state explicitly: if the Enlightenment speaks of universal natural rights, why does it exclude women? Her Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) applies Locke to the half of humanity he forgot.

df <- tibble::tribble(
  ~period,                ~gini, ~year,
  "Europe, 1500",          0.41,  1500,
  "Commercial expansion",  0.44,  1600,
  "Mercantilism",          0.46,  1700,
  "Eve of Ind. Rev.",      0.51,  1790
)
df_col <- tibble::tribble(
  ~region,          ~gini_est, ~period,
  "Colonial Carib.", 0.80, "1750",
  "Colonial Amer.",  0.65, "1750",
  "Europe",          0.46, "1750"
)
p <- ggplot2::ggplot(df, ggplot2::aes(x=year, y=gini, group=1,
    text=paste0(period,"<br>European Gini: ", gini))) +
  ggplot2::geom_line(color="#8B2020", linewidth=1) +
  ggplot2::geom_point(color="#8B2020", size=3) +
  ggplot2::scale_y_continuous(limits=c(.35,.85)) +
  ggplot2::geom_point(data=df_col,
    ggplot2::aes(x=as.numeric(period), y=gini_est, color=region,
    text=paste0(region,"<br>Estimated Gini: ", gini_est, "<br>(1750)")),
    size=5, shape=17) +
  ggplot2::scale_color_manual(values=c("Colonial Carib."="#6B0000","Colonial Amer."="#AA2020","Europe"="#2A5A8B")) +
  ggplot2::labs(x=NULL, y="Estimated Gini", color=NULL,
    caption="Sources: Milanovic & al. (2011); estimates for colonial economies") +
  ggplot2::theme_minimal(base_size=11, base_family="serif") +
  ggplot2::theme(panel.grid.minor=ggplot2::element_blank())
plotly::ggplotly(p, tooltip="text")

The triangles show statistical concealment: colonial economies are never aggregated into the ‘European’ Gini figures of the period.

Essential
Smith, A. — The Wealth of Nations, Books I and V (1776)
Read ch. 1 (division of labour) and ch. 1 of Book V (degradation of the worker).
Essential
Rousseau, J.-J. — Discourse on the Origin of Inequality (1755)
Second part — the most important. Short text, very readable.
In-depth
Williams, E. — Capitalism and Slavery (1944, University of North Carolina Press)

Culture

Applying Enlightenment natural rights to women — the founding text of liberal feminism.
Note

Connection to Act IV
Smith thought the division of labour created wealth. Ricardo and Marx would ask how that wealth is distributed between capitalists and workers — and whether this distribution is just or inevitably conflictual.
