What Act II left open
The Black Death showed that inequalities could recede mechanically, without revolution. Act III shows how, in the Renaissance and Enlightenment, inequality changes justification: no longer divine, it becomes economic — the result of progress and the division of labour.

Logbook · Lyon, July 1555
Lyon is the economic capital of the kingdom. Italian merchants trade promises rather than metals — the bill of exchange has replaced gold. I met the poet Louise Labé, daughter of an enriched ropemaker: free, in her own way, because her father had money.
Economic note The Lyon fair sees 50 tonnes of silver equivalent pass through annually. Meanwhile, 12 million Africans are deported to the Americas between 1500 and 1800 — unaccounted for by any Enlightenment economist.
Progress creates new freedoms for some — and new forms of servitude for others.

How inequality is perceived

From mercantilism to the Enlightenment, inequality changes justification: no longer divine, it becomes economic. It is the mechanical result of the division of labour, trade and private property.

But this rationalisation leaves immense blind spots: the Atlantic slave trade (12 million deported), the enclosure of commons in England, the exclusion of women from economic citizenship.

How it is justified

Thinker Thesis Blind spot
Adam Smith (1776) The invisible hand turns private interest into collective good He himself admits the degradation of the specialised worker (Book V)
Locke (1689) Property arises from labour — therefore legitimate Ignores colonial expropriation of land
Rousseau (1755) Moral/political inequality is constructed, not natural Proposes no concrete economic reform
Quesnay (1758) Wealth comes from land; other classes are sterile Underestimates trade and industry
Mandeville (1714) Private vices make public wealth Cynical legitimation of inequalities

Women’s Thread — Act III

Louise Labé (1524–1566), Lyonnaise poet, embodies the contradiction: exceptional intellectual freedom, acquired through her father’s merchant wealth, in a society that excluded women from guilds, universities and official commerce. Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797) would be the first to state explicitly: if the Enlightenment speaks of universal natural rights, why does it exclude women? Her Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) applies Locke to the half of humanity he forgot.

Further reading — Act III
Essential
Smith, A. — The Wealth of Nations, Books I and V (1776)
Read ch. 1 (division of labour) and ch. 1 of Book V (degradation of the worker).
Essential
Rousseau, J.-J. — Discourse on the Origin of Inequality (1755)
Second part — the most important. Short text, very readable.
In-depth
Williams, E. — Capitalism and Slavery (1944, University of North Carolina Press)
The founding thesis: the British Industrial Revolution was financed by the slave trade.
Culture
Wollstonecraft, M. — A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792)
Applying Enlightenment natural rights to women — the founding text of liberal feminism.
Note

Connection to Act IV
Smith thought the division of labour created wealth. Ricardo and Marx would ask how that wealth is distributed between capitalists and workers — and whether this distribution is just or inevitably conflictual.
