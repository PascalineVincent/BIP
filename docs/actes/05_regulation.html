What Act IV left open

How inequality is perceived

The 20th century is paradoxical: it is simultaneously the century of the greatest catastrophes produced by inequalities (world wars, genocides, crises) and the century of the greatest documented compression of inequalities.

The parenthesis of the Glorious Thirty (1945–1975) is historically exceptional. It results from a rare conjunction: wealth destruction by two wars, highly progressive taxation, mass unionisation, and the welfare state. Piketty insists: without these political counter-forces, r > g reasserts itself.

How it is justified, fought and reduced

Thinker Position Instrument
Keynes (1936) Reform Effective demand — the state as lender of last resort
Beveridge (1942) Reform Universal welfare state — from cradle to grave
Hayek (1944) Liberal Redistribution = serfdom; the market = liberty
Kuznets (1955) Optimistic Growth spontaneously reduces inequalities (inverted U)
Friedman (1962) Liberal Negative income tax — the only acceptable redistribution
Piketty (2013) Critical r > g — structural tendency toward wealth concentration

Women’s Thread — Act V

Joan Robinson (1903–1983): member of Keynes’s Cambridge Circus, co-author of the Keynesian revolution, pioneer of imperfect competition (1933). She was expected to receive the Nobel Prize in 1975 when Hayek was awarded it — she never received it. A paradigmatic case of gender bias in Nobel economics awards (fewer than 5% women laureates since 1969). Rosa Parks (1913–2005) and the civil rights movement show that the American Glorious Thirty systematically exclude Black Americans — segregation, discrimination in the New Deal, banking redlining.
