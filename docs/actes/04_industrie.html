Logbook · Manchester, 15 March 1848
Smoke, the noise of machines, children working from the age of six. The factories run for 14 hours a day. Workers return in the evening to lightless alleyways where life expectancy is 28 years. A man gave me a hastily printed text: The Communist Manifesto. He explains that the capitalist legally robs the worker. I don't know if it's true. But the figures from the parliamentary inquiry into child labour are chilling.
Economic note Average weekly wage of a textile worker in Manchester in 1848: 7 shillings. Rent for an insalubrious room: 2 shillings. Minimum food: 4 shillings. Left over: 1 shilling. Wage of a mill director: £15 per week (60× the worker's wage). Ricardo calls this a 'natural law of wages'.
Industrial inequality is not invisible like that of Antiquity. It pours out of chimneys, crowds into alleyways, and for the first time it is frontally challenged as a system — not as an accident.

How inequality is perceived

In the 19th century, inequality ceases to be invisible. It piles up in cities, pours out of chimneys, is documented by parliamentary inquiries, novels and nascent statistics. And for the first time, it is frontally challenged as a system.

Marx does not say poverty is sad — he says it is structurally produced by capitalism. This changes entirely what political response is possible.

Women’s Thread — Act IV

Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919): the first woman to produce a major theoretical work in Marxist economics. The Accumulation of Capital (1913) anticipates Keynes on effective demand and grounds imperialism in economic necessity. Beatrice Potter Webb (1858–1943): co-founder of the London School of Economics, pioneer of fieldwork on working conditions. Her research on cooperatives and trade unions shapes British labourism.
