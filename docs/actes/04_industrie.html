What Act III left open
Adam Smith claimed that the invisible hand transforms individual self-interest into collective good. But he himself acknowledged that the division of labour degrades the worker. Act IV shows how Ricardo, Marx and their contemporaries transform this tension into a theory of conflict.

Smoke, noise, children working from the age of six. A man handed me the freshly printed Communist Manifesto and explained that the capitalist legally robs the worker.
Economic note Textile worker weekly wage: 7 shillings. Rent: 2s. Food: 4s. Left over: 1 shilling. A mill director earns £15/week — 60 times more.
Industrial inequality is the first to be frontally challenged as a system, not an accident.

How inequality is perceived

In the 19th century, inequality ceases to be invisible. It piles up in cities, pours out of chimneys, is documented by parliamentary inquiries, novels and nascent statistics. And for the first time, it is frontally challenged as a system.

Marx does not say poverty is sad — he says it is structurally produced by capitalism. This changes entirely what political response is possible.

How it is justified and contested

Thinker Position Key_argument
Ricardo Justifies Iron law of wages — tend toward subsistence minimum
Malthus Justifies Poverty inevitable — population grows faster than resources
Marx Contests Surplus value = legalised exploitation; capital is stolen labour
Engels Contests Fieldwork inquiry (Condition of the Working Class, 1845)
Mill Reforms Tempered liberalism — redistribution possible without destroying liberty
Proudhon Contests ‘Property is theft’ — economic anarchism

Women’s Thread — Act IV

Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919): the first woman to produce a major theoretical work in Marxist economics. The Accumulation of Capital (1913) anticipates Keynes on effective demand and grounds imperialism in economic necessity. Beatrice Potter Webb (1858–1943): co-founder of the London School of Economics, pioneer of fieldwork on working conditions. Her research on cooperatives and trade unions shapes British labourism.

Ch. 4The Classics (I): Smith, Ricardo and the Labour Theory of Value (Ricardo)Session 4

Ricardo (1817) corrects Smith’s measure of value and builds the first theory of distribution among wages, profit and rent.

Key ideas from the course

  • Ricardo’s critique: value depends on labour embodied in production, not on the labour a good can command.
  • Distribution is the central question: what rises for one class falls for another.
  • Profit, accumulation and the stationary state: diminishing returns on land raise rent and squeeze profit until accumulation stops.
  • Real wage data (course): the real wage index rises only slowly over the industrial century (1750 = 42, 1890 = 102, 1900 = 100), while the Act IV Gini rises from .50 (1800) to .58 in the UK by 1860.

Models and formulas

Value = labour embodiedRent from diminishing returns on landStationary state

Ch. 5The Classics (II): Malthus, Mill, Say and the German Historical SchoolSession 5

A century of debates: population, poor relief, outlets, and whether a universal economic theory exists.

Key ideas from the course

  • Malthus’s mechanism: population grows faster than food unless checked, so poor relief only raises numbers (the Poor Laws debate).
  • Say’s law and the controversy over outlets: supply creates its own demand; Malthus and Sismondi contest it, anticipating Keynes.
  • Mill (1848): separates economic science (what is) from political decision (what ought to be); his programme includes progressive land taxation, high inheritance duties, workers’ cooperatives and votes for women.
  • Von Thünen is the first to use the differential tool in economics; Bastiat is a radical liberal polemicist; List defends protection of national industry.
  • German Historical School: a method of history over deduction, ending in the Methodenstreit (1883–84) against Menger.

Models and formulas

Say’s law: supply creates its own demandMalthus: population ↑ geometrically, food ↑ arithmeticallyVon Thünen: interest rate = (P − A)/A; natural wage = √((P − a)·a)

Authors to know

Ch. 6Marx and SocialismSession 6

The “social question” turns inequality into a conflict between classes, and socialist thought becomes a rival tradition.

Key ideas from the course

  • Precursors: Rousseau, Babeuf (the first communist), Sismondi (theorist of crises), the Ricardian and utopian socialists.
  • Marx’s approach: historical materialism, class struggle, dialectics.
  • Empirical datum: Engels measured in Manchester (1845) a working-class life expectancy of 28 years against 38 for the middle classes.
  • Surplus value and alienation: profit comes from surplus value (Pl); if the exploitation rate is constant and mechanisation raises q, the profit rate tends to fall.
  • Capital (1867) and the role of money.
  • 1848 in action: Louis Blanc and Proudhon.
  • Contemporary tests: Piketty on concentration, Saito on Marx as ecologist, Dardot–Laval and Ostrom on the common.

Models and formulas

Rate of exploitation e = Pl / CvOrganic composition q = Cf / CvProfit rate π = Pl / (Cf + Cv) = e / (1 + q)

Ch. 7Marginalism and the NeoclassicalsSession 7

The 1870s revolution changes the unit of analysis: from classes to individual choices at the margin.

Key ideas from the course

  • Subjective value: price reflects marginal utility, not labour (Condillac, Jevons, Menger, Walras).
  • Precursors: Cournot (price elasticity of demand), Dupuit (consumer surplus), Bentham (utilitarianism).
  • Reasoning “as in physics”: mathematics and equilibrium become the language of the discipline.
  • Classicals vs neoclassicals: distribution gives way to allocation; the social question fades from the core of theory.
  • Two limits of utilitarianism: utility cannot be compared across persons, and the sum of utilities ignores who bears the cost.

Models and formulas

Marginal utility, U′(x)Price elasticity ε = (∂Q/Q) / (∂p/p)General equilibrium (Walras)

Authors to know

Ch. 8The Affirmation of the Neoclassicals: Marshall, Edgeworth, Pigou, ParetoSession 8

Cambridge and Lausanne give the neoclassical programme its tools: supply and demand, indifference curves, externalities, the Pareto optimum.

Key ideas from the course

  • Marshall (Principles, 1890): supply and demand as a cross, partial equilibrium, consumer surplus, economies of scale.
  • Edgeworth: indifference curves and the Edgeworth box.
  • Pigou: welfare economics and the Pigouvian tax on externalities.
  • Pareto: the 20/80 law, ordinal utility and the Pareto optimum; silent on distribution.
  • Link to Act V: Pigou’s wage flexibility is what Keynes attacks in 1936.

Models and formulas

Pareto optimum: nobody can gain without someone losingPigouvian tax = marginal external costEdgeworth box

Authors to know

MarshallEdgeworthPigouPareto

Connection to Act V
Marx predicted the collapse of capitalism through its internal contradictions. In the 20th century, it does not collapse — it is regulated. Keynes, the welfare state and trade unions produce the compression of inequalities of the Glorious Thirty. Act V shows how and why.
