What Act I left open
Aristotle justified inequality through nature — some are born to command. But he also condemned chrematistics. Act II shows how the feudal order takes up this framework and sacralises it: the hierarchy is no longer natural — it is willed by God.

Logbook · Paris, January 1349
The Plague killed a third of the city in six months. The survivors who worked yesterday for almost nothing are suddenly demanding double. The lords call it ingratitude. I call it economics.
Economic note Before the Plague: 1 denier/day for an agricultural labourer. After (1380): 3–4 deniers. The Ordinances of 1351 tried to freeze wages — in vain.
Even a catastrophe can reduce inequalities — if it redistributes the balance of power.

How inequality is perceived

The feudal order produces a radically different justification from Antiquity: inequality is no longer natural — it is theological. God willed the three orders — those who pray, those who fight, those who work — and to question this is to question divine will.

Thomas Aquinas takes up Aristotle but integrates him into a Christian framework: earthly hierarchy reflects celestial order. Inequality is providentially useful.

Women’s Thread — Act II

Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179): abbess, composer, naturalist, theologian. Her case illustrates the medieval paradox: the convent as a space of intellectual autonomy for elite women, but a space entirely dependent on male ecclesiastical authority. The exception proves the rule: gender inequality is as ontological as status inequality.

The data — The Black Death effect

The demographic shock of the Black Death produces a compression of inequalities — rare in pre-industrial history.

Question 1 · The Plague as economic laboratory
To help you write
  • In what sense does the Black Death constitute a 'natural experiment' for economists?
  • Why did lords try to block wages by law? Did it work?
  • Can you think of other catastrophes that have redistributed economic power?

Essential
Ibn Khaldun — Muqaddimah (1377), ch. II and III
The first theory of economic and political cycles. Available in Franz Rosenthal's English translation.

The economic and social effects of the Plague. Revises the classic narrative.

Note

Connection to the next act
The Black Death showed something unsettling for the powerful: inequalities can shrink without revolution, simply through a demographic shock. Act III will show how Renaissance humanists begin to think of inequality as an effect of progress — no longer as divine or natural order.
