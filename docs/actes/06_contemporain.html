What Act V left open
The Glorious Thirty proved that inequalities could be sustainably reduced. But this reduction rested on a set of political counter-forces that the neo-liberal turn of the 1980s progressively dismantled. Act VI shows where we stand — and why it is difficult.

How inequality is perceived

The paradox of the 21st century: we have never measured inequalities so thoroughly (WID.world, Oxfam, OECD, public databases) — and they have never seemed so hard to reduce.

Piketty provides the framework: r > g — the return on capital exceeds the growth rate, wealth accumulates structurally. But Milanovic complicates the picture: globally, inequalities between countries have fallen (thanks to the rise of China and Asia). It is inequalities within rich countries that are exploding.

Women’s Thread — Act VI (synthesis)

Fewer than 20% of economics professors are women in most countries. Three women have received the Nobel Prize in economics: Elinor Ostrom (2009, commons), Esther Duflo (2019, poverty), Claudia Goldin (2023, labour market). Final question: would economics be different if women had not been systematically excluded since Aristotle? What questions were never asked? What data was never collected?

The elephant curve

Milanovic’s ‘elephant curve’: the big winners from globalisation are the emerging middle classes (the back) and the global top 1% (the trunk). The lower-middle classes of rich countries (the dip) have stagnated — which partly explains the rise of populisms.
