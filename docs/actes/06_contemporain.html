Logbook · San Francisco, April 2026
Two worlds on the same street. On one side, the Big Tech campuses — green spaces, free cafeterias, six-figure salaries. On the other, tents on the pavements of Market Street. An Uber driver explains his algorithm to me. He works 10 hours a day, earns $1,200 a month, has no health insurance, and is legally 'independent'. Aristotle would have called that a slave. Smith would have called that a free worker. Marx would have called that precarious labour. I no longer know who is right.
Economic note In 2025, the top 1% in the United States hold 38% of national wealth. Life expectancy varies by 20 years depending on the zip code in major American cities. Meanwhile, globalisation has lifted 1 billion people out of absolute poverty in Asia — but deepened inequalities within rich countries. This is Milanovic's elephant curve: everyone has gained, except the middle classes of rich countries.
25 centuries later, the question has not changed: who decides on value? Who produces it? Who benefits from it? And who has the right to ask these questions?

How inequality is perceived

The paradox of the 21st century: we have never measured inequalities so thoroughly (WID.world, Oxfam, OECD, public databases) — and they have never seemed so hard to reduce.

Piketty provides the framework: r > g — the return on capital exceeds the growth rate, wealth accumulates structurally. But Milanovic complicates the picture: globally, inequalities between countries have fallen (thanks to the rise of China and Asia). It is inequalities within rich countries that are exploding.
